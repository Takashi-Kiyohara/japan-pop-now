The first time I fed a 300-yen gachapon machine in Akihabara, I got the same SPY x FAMILY figure three turns in a row. A woman next to me got the rare Yor Forger on her first try, smiled politely, and walked away. I stared at my three identical Anyas and realised nobody had told me the rules of this game.

That is the experience of every first-timer. Japan has an estimated 600,000 of them. The market hit 610 billion yen in 2023 and is still growing. They look chaotic but there is a logic to it, and once you understand the logic you stop getting triple Anyas.

This guide is the walkthrough I wish I had on that first trip. What to bring, how each machine actually works, where the best hunting grounds are in Tokyo, which series are worth your coins in 2026, and the five mistakes I see tourists make over and over. No fluff, written by someone who has probably spent more on gachapon than is wise.

## What Is Gachapon? A Quick Primer

The word *gachapon* comes from two sounds: *gacha* (the crank turning) and *pon* (the capsule dropping). You will also see it written as *gashapon* (Bandai's trademarked version) or just *gacha*. They all mean the same thing — coin-operated capsule toy dispensers that sell licensed figures, miniature food replicas, acrylic charms, and increasingly art-toy crossovers.

What started in the 1960s as cheap plastic toys for kids has turned into a serious collectibles market. Today's lineup includes museum-quality miniature ramen bowls, functional phone accessories, Schleich animal collaborations, and design-studio pieces that show up in interior magazines. The capsules are still random — you drop coins, you turn the crank, you get what the machine decides to give you — but the things inside have become genuinely well-made.

The detail that trips up every first-time visitor: Japanese gachapon almost always sell in themed sets of four to six designs. Each capsule inside a given machine is random, so on a six-design set you have a one-in-six chance of getting the specific figure you want. Every machine has a printed lineup card on the front showing all possible results. Learn to read that card before you insert coins and you will already be ahead of 90 per cent of tourists.

## Preparation: Before You Turn a Single Crank

Three things to do before you start hunting:

**1. Get 100-yen coins.** Most gachapon machines in Tokyo still only accept 100-yen coins. A few newer digital machines in big shops like Gachapon no Mori accept IC cards (Suica, PASMO, ICOCA), but assume coins. Bank ATMs only dispense 1,000-yen bills. The easy fix: buy a drink at a FamilyMart or Lawson and ask for change, or walk into any dedicated gachapon shop and use the ryougae-ki (change machine) near the entrance. Bring 2,000 yen worth of coins for a fun session.

**2. Decide a spending cap before you walk in.** Gachapon math is dangerous. One capsule costs 300 yen, but "just one more turn" is how you end up 3,000 yen deep with four duplicate Anyas. Set a hard limit out loud — 1,500 yen, 2,000 yen, whatever feels right — and stop when you hit it. The machines are designed to make stopping hard.

**3. Know what you actually want.** Walking into Gachapon no Mori with 500 machines in front of you and no plan is the fastest way to overspend. Before you go, bookmark one or two series you are actively hunting (a specific anime, a miniature food line, an animal figure set) and a loose category you are open to (keychains, charms, figures). With a rough target in mind, you can filter 500 machines down to ten in about five minutes.

## How to Use a Gachapon Machine, Step by Step

Once you have coins and a target, the mechanics take about fifteen seconds per turn.

1. **Check the price on the lineup card.** Prices are printed on a sticker or the printed card on the front of the machine. 200, 300, 400, or 500 yen depending on the series and figure quality. Look for the number before you reach for coins.
2. **Count the lineup.** The lineup card shows every possible result in the set, usually four to six designs. Look at them. If you only want one specific figure out of six, know that you are accepting a one-in-six chance of getting it.
3. **Insert the coins one at a time.** Feed 100-yen coins into the slot. Most machines have a small counter above the slot that shows how much you have inserted. Keep feeding until it matches the price.
4. **Turn the crank fully clockwise.** A complete rotation. If the crank refuses to turn, you have not inserted enough coins — do not force it. Add another coin and try again.
5. **Grab your capsule from the drop tray.** It will land in the plastic tray at the bottom of the machine. Open the capsule to see what you got.
6. **Recycle the empty shell.** Every dedicated gachapon location has a capsule-recycling bin nearby. Please use it. Leaving empty shells on the floor is how tourist behaviour gets talked about on Japanese Twitter, and it is an easy thing to get right.

One critical rule: **these machines do not give change.** If a capsule costs 300 yen, you need exactly three 100-yen coins. No bills, no 50-yen coins, no credit cards on most machines. Convenience store cashiers will usually not break a bill just for change, but buying a small item (a drink, a rice ball) makes the transaction natural.

## Where to Find the Best Gachapon in Tokyo

Gachapon machines are everywhere, but a few locations are worth making a specific trip for.

### Dedicated Gachapon Shops

**Gachapon no Mori (Akihabara).** Multiple floors of nothing but gachapon machines — 500-plus at any given time, constantly rotated with new releases. This is the single best place in Tokyo if you want maximum selection. Located right on Chuo-dori, impossible to miss.

| --- | --- |
| **Station** | JR Akihabara Electric Town Exit, 3-minute walk |
| **Hours** | 10:00 – 22:00 daily |
| **Machines** | 500+ |

**Gashapon Bandai Official Shop (Tokyo Station).** Bandai's own store inside Tokyo Station Character Street on the underground B1 floor. Smaller than Akihabara shops but focuses on Bandai-exclusive releases and the occasional Tokyo Station limited edition. Convenient if you are transferring trains anyway.

**Gashapon Department Store (Ikebukuro Sunshine City).** Opened in 2022 and still one of the largest gachapon spaces in Japan with around 3,000 machines. If you are in Ikebukuro, this replaces Akihabara as your gachapon destination. Pairs well with Animate Ikebukuro and Otome Road — see the [Ikebukuro Anime Guide](/articles/ikebukuro-anime-guide-2026) for the whole neighbourhood circuit.

**Kenelephant Gachapon Department (Shibuya).** Inside Shibuya PARCO. Known for art-toy crossovers and trendy indie collaborations. Smaller footprint but a hand-picked selection that skews toward the premium 400-500 yen range.

### Big Retail Stores With Large Gachapon Sections

**Yodobashi Camera Akihabara.** The 6th-floor toy section has 200-plus machines lined up along a wall. Solid anime selection. The practical advantage: you are already inside a store that accepts credit cards, so you can buy a drink or a small item and use the change machine without hunting for a konbini first.

**Animate (all locations).** Every Animate store has a gachapon corner near the entrance or on a dedicated floor. The Ikebukuro flagship has the largest anime-focused selection of the chain.

**Don Quijote.** The discount chain keeps 50 to 100 machines on the ground floor of most locations. Selection varies but the Shibuya mega-store and Akihabara branch are the best of the network. Open 24 hours in most cities — the only way to hunt gachapon at 3 a.m.

### Everyday Spots (Free, No Detour Needed)

You do not need to visit a specialty shop to find gachapon. Machines appear in everyday locations across Japan: train station concourses (especially JR stations), shopping mall entrances, convenience store fronts (FamilyMart and Lawson most consistently), airport departure lounges in Narita and Haneda, and the basement floors of department stores. If you are walking around Tokyo and paying attention, you will pass a machine roughly every ten minutes.

## Best Series and Brands to Look For in 2026

Not all gachapon are created equal. Four brands consistently produce quality worth your coins.

**Bandai (Gashapon).** The biggest name in the game. Bandai's capsule division produces most of the anime-licensed figures you will see — their HG (High Grade) and Gashapon Collection lines are particularly good. If it is a Jump manga character, Bandai probably has a gashapon version.

**Takara Tomy Arts.** Known for clever, functional pieces. Their miniature appliance series — tiny working fans, miniature vending machines, small arcade cabinets — went viral internationally in 2022 and they are still releasing new entries. Also produces excellent animal figurine lines.

**Qualia.** The premium indie brand. Qualia makes miniature food replicas that are almost uncomfortably realistic. Their sushi, ramen, and convenience-store bento lines are display-worthy. Typically 400-500 yen, but the quality earns it.

**Kitan Club.** Masters of "weird but charming". If you have seen photos of a cat wearing a banana hat or a frog sitting on a mushroom, that is probably Kitan Club. Their Cup no Fuchiko (the figure that hangs on the rim of a cup) became a cultural phenomenon and is still in rotation with new collaborations.

### Hot Series in Early 2026

Anime gachapon cycles fast, but the series currently taking up the most shelf space in Tokyo shops as of April 2026 are Jujutsu Kaisen (new movie tie-ins), One Piece (Gear 5 Luffy figures sell out fastest), SPY x FAMILY (Anya in every possible pose), Chiikawa (Japan's current mascot obsession — expect actual queues at Chiikawa machines), and Demon Slayer (the Hashira collection is still rolling). Beyond anime, look for the miniature food replica lines and the animal figurine series — those are the ones that tend to translate well as gifts for non-anime friends.

## 5 Common Mistakes First-Timers Make

These are the specific errors I see tourists repeat at Gachapon no Mori every single visit. Avoid them and your session will be smoother than mine was.

**1. Spending all your 100-yen coins at one machine chasing a single figure.** This is the Anya mistake I made. If you want a specific figure out of a six-design set, the expected cost to pull it is around 1,800 yen (six turns), and the variance is brutal — you might spend 3,000 yen and still not get it. If you absolutely need one specific figure, it is cheaper to turn the machine once, accept whatever you get, and buy the figure you actually want on Mercari or Suruga-ya. Both have second-hand gachapon sections where individual capsule contents sell for 300 to 1,200 yen.

**2. Not checking the lineup card for crossed-out designs.** At dedicated shops, staff often mark the lineup card to show which figures have already been pulled from the remaining capsules in that machine. A card with four of six designs crossed out means the remaining capsules only contain the other two. If the remaining two are both figures you want, turn the crank. If they are the rejects, walk away. The card tells you everything.

**3. Buying gachapon at the airport on the way out.** Narita and Haneda both have machines in the duty-free area, and the prices are slightly inflated while the selection is smaller. If you are thinking "I will just get them at the airport", reconsider — you will pay more for less. Buy in the city the day before and pack the capsules in your carry-on.

**4. Turning the crank before checking the price.** A surprisingly common small mistake. 300-yen and 400-yen machines sit next to each other, and it is easy to insert three coins and turn confidently at a 400-yen machine. The crank will not budge, you will spend thirty seconds confused, and then you will walk to a ryougae-ki to get another 100-yen coin. Save yourself the step. Look at the price first.

**5. Ignoring the duplicate problem.** If you pull a duplicate on turn two of a six-design set, the odds of pulling another duplicate on turn three are roughly one in three. Duplicates compound fast. The fix: bring a Japanese friend or a trading-board at the shop (Gachapon no Mori and some Animate locations have boards where visitors swap duplicates for free). Or embrace it — gift the duplicates to friends back home who will not care that you got the same one twice.

## 5 Best Gachapon Picks for First-Time Visitors

With thousands of machines to choose from, these five categories consistently delight first-time visitors.

1. **Miniature Japanese food replicas (300 yen).** Tiny sushi, ramen bowls, and bento boxes that look almost real. Qualia and Kitan Club make the best ones. Perfect desk decoration and universally understood gifts — you do not need to know anime for a mini ramen bowl to be charming.
2. **Anime character swing keychains (300 yen).** Small enough to attach to your bag or phone case. Every major series has a swing keychain set. Bandai dominates this category and their quality is the most consistent.
3. **Cup no Fuchiko rim hangers (300 yen).** Figures designed to perch on the edge of a cup or glass. Kitan Club started this trend and it is still going with new character collaborations. Works as both a desk toy and a conversation piece at dinner.
4. **Animal figurines (400 yen).** Detailed sleeping cats, sitting dogs, wildlife. These transcend anime fandom. The Takara Tomy Arts sleeping animal series and Ikimon Nature Techni Color line are the two to look for specifically.
5. **Miniature electronics and appliances (500 yen).** Tiny working fans, miniature vending machines, small arcade cabinets. Takara Tomy Arts makes most of these. They are conversation starters and the only gachapon category that tends to make people laugh out loud when they open the capsule.

**Budget guide.** 2,000 yen in 100-yen coins is the right amount for a satisfying first session. That gets you four to six capsules across different machines — enough variety to feel you explored, not so much that you drown in duplicates.

## Pro Tips From a Capsule Toy Addict

**Hit the shops Saturday morning.** Dedicated gachapon shops rotate stock throughout the week, but the biggest lineup refreshes happen Friday evening. Visit Saturday morning for first pick at newly-loaded machines.

**Weekend crowds are real at Chiikawa machines.** If you are specifically hunting Chiikawa or a hot new series, Saturday afternoon at Gachapon no Mori means fifteen-minute waits at the popular machines. Weekday mornings have no lines.

**Look for "last one" machines.** Machines near the end of a run sometimes have only one to three capsules left, and the lineup card shows which designs remain. If only the figures you want remain, that is a guaranteed win for less than the cost of gambling at a fresh machine.

**Duplicates are tradeable or resellable.** Hold onto duplicates. Some gachapon shops have trading boards where collectors swap duplicates for free. Mercari is where serious collectors liquidate theirs after a trip — a popular duplicate figure will sell for roughly 500 yen on average, which recovers most of the turn cost.

**Check the Mercari search before you overspend.** If you are chasing a specific figure and you are already at turn three with no luck, pause and check the Mercari app. A specific figure from a released set usually sells second-hand for 300 to 1,200 yen. If the Mercari price is lower than your expected remaining cost to chase, just buy it online and stop turning.

**Customs-friendly souvenirs.** Gachapon capsules are small, lightweight, non-food, and non-electronic. They sail through customs in any country, pack flat in carry-on, and cost under five dollars each. For souvenir-per-yen value, gachapon is one of the best choices in Japan, as long as you respect your spending cap.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use credit cards or IC cards at gachapon machines?

Most gachapon machines are coin-only and require 100-yen coins. However, a growing number of digital machines in major shops — Gachapon no Mori in Akihabara and Gashapon Department Store in Ikebukuro Sunshine City are the two best examples — now accept IC cards (Suica, PASMO, ICOCA). Dedicated gachapon shops always have change machines nearby if you only have bills.

### How much should I budget for gachapon?

Most capsules cost 200 to 500 yen (about 1.30 to 3.30 US dollars at current rates). A fun session of five to six turns runs 1,500 to 2,000 yen (about 10 to 13 US dollars). Serious collectors can easily spend 5,000 yen in one visit. Set a hard cap before you walk in — this is the most important rule of gachapon hunting.

### Can I bring gachapon toys through airport customs?

Yes. Gachapon capsule toys are small, non-food plastic items with no customs restrictions in any country I am aware of. They are one of the most travel-friendly souvenirs you can buy in Japan. Pack the capsules in carry-on luggage to prevent damage from checked bag handling.

### Where is the best place for gachapon in Tokyo?

Gachapon no Mori in Akihabara has 500-plus machines and the widest general selection. Gashapon Department Store in Ikebukuro Sunshine City is newer and actually larger at roughly 3,000 machines, making it the best destination if you are already in Ikebukuro. For premium and trendy indie lines, Kenelephant Gachapon Department in Shibuya PARCO has a smaller but hand-picked selection.

### Are gachapon machines only in Tokyo?

No. Japan has over 600,000 gachapon machines nationwide. Every major city has them — Osaka's Den Den Town and Kyoto Station both have excellent dedicated shops. You will find machines in every train station, shopping mall, and convenience store entrance across the country.
